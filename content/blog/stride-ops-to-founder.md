I’m a builder first, but my path to **ClassQuip** ran through the unglamorous side of edtech: keeping live programs running when a parent’s VPN, a frozen Chromebook, or a misconfigured roster threatened an entire class period.

At **Stride / K12**, I saw how districts actually use virtual learning—not how slide decks say they do.

## Operations is a product discipline

Support and ops work is often treated as cost center work. For me it was a continuous user research program:

- Which failure modes teachers learn to work around instead of reporting?
- Where does “the platform said they attended” disagree with what the teacher saw?
- What do admins export at 4 p.m. on Friday because the state auditor calls on Monday?

Those questions don’t show up in a Jira ticket titled “optimize WebRTC.” They show up as **trust erosion**—teachers stop relying on video, students disengage, and districts renew the wrong vendor because switching feels harder than suffering.

## The founder decision

Starting ClassQuip wasn’t a leap away from education; it was a bet that the next layer of the stack had to be **purpose-built for K–12 constraints** instead of repurposed enterprise conferencing.

I wanted:

- Infrastructure I could reason about when a session drops at 8:07 a.m. Eastern.
- Pricing that doesn’t punish a district for turning video on for every section.
- Integrations that meet schools where their rosters and LMS already live.

CEO title aside, I still spend most of my time in architecture and code—media paths, integrations, and the unsexy reliability work that lets teachers teach.

## Still in Knoxville

I’m based in **Knoxville, TN**. Same town, same mission: software that works in real classrooms. This blog and [my GitHub](https://github.com/buhlaustin) are where the side quests live; ClassQuip is the main quest.

If your world overlaps—WebRTC, K–12, or founder-stage edtech—reach out. I learn faster in conversation than in isolation.
